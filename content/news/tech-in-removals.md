Not so long ago, planning an office move ran on spreadsheets, long email chains and a good deal of guesswork. Someone counted desks on a printout, someone else rang the removal company to confirm the date again, and nobody knew which boxes were in which van until they were opened.

Most of that has changed. The physical work is the same as ever: lifting, wrapping, loading and driving through London traffic. What has changed is the information around it, and the result is fewer surprises, less downtime and a clearer record of what happened. Here are the tools that matter for an office move and where they make a real difference.

## Quotes and surveys without waiting for a site visit

The quote used to be the slowest part. A surveyor had to find a slot, travel to your office, walk the floor and send an estimate days later.

Online quote forms shorten that first step: two addresses, a date and a rough size are enough to start a conversation. Video surveys have replaced many site visits altogether. You walk through the office on a phone, the surveyor asks you to open the comms room door or show the goods lift, and the survey is done in twenty minutes. A large or complex office still justifies an in-person survey, but a video call is usually enough for a smaller one.

## One shared move plan instead of fifty emails

An office move involves your facilities lead, your IT team, both landlords, the removal company and often a fit-out contractor. When each pair talks by email, nobody has the full picture.

A shared move plan fixes this. It lives in one place, everyone involved can see it, and when a date or a layout changes, it changes once. Good plans are organised as checklists with owners and deadlines:

- Notice periods, lease dates and handover inspections
- Parking suspensions, loading bay bookings and goods lift reservations
- Building management requirements such as a method statement and risk assessment
- IT shutdown and restart sequence
- Who is on site at each address and when
- What moves, what goes to storage and what gets recycled

The checklist does not need to be sophisticated. A document everyone can edit beats a project tool that only one person opens.

## Labelling, inventory and crate tracking

Handwritten labels fade, fall off and get misread. "Finance 3rd floor" means nothing to a crew member working from a floor plan with desk numbers.

Barcode and QR code labels solve both problems. Each crate or item gets a unique code that is scanned when it is packed, scanned when it is loaded and scanned again when it is placed at the new office. The scan ties the object to a record: what is inside, where it came from, where it is going and who packed it. If a crate goes missing, the last scan tells you where it was seen. For an office manager that means:

- Every crate has a destination, so nothing sits in a corridor waiting for a decision
- Archive boxes and confidential files are tracked box by box
- The inventory doubles as a packing list for insurance

The same tracking solves the oldest problem with hired plastic crates: getting them back. A crate record shows how many went to each floor and how many have come back, so stragglers are chased before the hire period ends rather than found in a stationery cupboard three months later. ## Route planning and live arrival updates

London traffic is the biggest variable on move day. A crew leaving for the City at six in the morning has a very different journey from one leaving at nine.

Route planning software accounts for road closures, low emission zone boundaries, height and weight restrictions. Live tracking then lets the move coordinator see where each vehicle is and share an arrival window with the person waiting at the new building. That matters: the goods lift may be booked for a two-hour slot, the building manager may need to open a loading bay, and your IT team may be waiting to start. A message saying the first van is twenty minutes away is worth more than any reassurance the day before.

## Photo records for condition and insurance

Damage disputes used to come down to memory. Was that scratch on the boardroom table there before the move?

Phones have made photo records standard. Crews photograph furniture and equipment before wrapping, photograph the empty office at handover, and photograph items in position at the new address. Time-stamped images attached to the inventory give both sides a clear record. Claims are settled from evidence rather than argument, and the landlord inspection at the old building is easier when you can show how you left it.

## Digital floor plans and desk allocation

The destination floor plan is where most office moves go wrong. Furniture arrives, nobody knows where desk 42 is, and the crew stacks everything in the middle of the room to be sorted later. That "later" is your staff's first morning in the new office.

Digital floor plans give each desk, meeting room and storage unit a number, and the labelling system uses the same numbers. The crew places a crate by reading its code and matching it to the plan on a tablet. Desk allocation tools go a step further, linking each employee to a desk so their monitor, chair and crates land in the right place. For hybrid offices with fewer desks than staff, the same tools help decide what furniture is needed at the new site and what can go to storage or be recycled.

## Keeping IT equipment and data secure

IT is the highest-risk part of any office move, and technology helps protect both the hardware and the data on it.

On the hardware side, anti-static crates, labelled cable bundles and a scan at every handover mean equipment is tracked as closely as any other asset and placed first at the new address, so reconnection can begin while furniture is still arriving. On the data side, the steps are about process: back everything up before the move, encrypt laptops and drives, keep a signed-for list of any device that holds company or customer data, and make sure decommissioned equipment is wiped and recycled by a registered waste carrier with the paperwork to prove it, not left in a skip. Anything that needs to sit between offices for a while belongs in [secure storage](/storage/) rather than a spare room with a broken lock.

## What still needs people

For all of this, software does not move an office. People do.

A barcode will tell you where a crate is, but it will not notice that the goods lift is out of order and work out a new sequence. A route planner will not talk the building manager into holding the loading bay a little longer. A floor plan will not spot that the new meeting room door is narrower than the table, which is why a good surveyor still measures. And no tool replaces a named move coordinator who answers the phone when the plan changes the day before.

The best office moves use digital tools to remove the guesswork and paperwork so experienced people can concentrate on the judgement calls.
