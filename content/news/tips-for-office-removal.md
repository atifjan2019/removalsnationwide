Relocating an office rarely feels straightforward. The plan can look complete on paper, with a date agreed and a floor plan signed off, yet the reality tends to involve tight deadlines, details that nobody owned and the pressure of keeping the business running while everything around it is packed into crates. Most of that stress is predictable, and what is predictable can be planned for.

The thirteen tips below make the biggest difference for London office managers and business owners. Work through them in order and the move becomes a project rather than a scramble.

## 1. Start early and set a timeline

The most common cause of a difficult office move is starting too late. Lease dates are fixed, building managers need notice and suppliers book up, so the earlier you begin, the more options you keep. For a small office, two to three months is comfortable. For a multi-floor move with a comms room, start six months out.

Write the timeline down and work backwards from move day, marking when the survey must be done, the quote accepted, the crates delivered, the IT plan agreed and the staff briefed. One shared document is far easier to keep to than several people's memories.

## 2. Appoint a move lead

Every office move needs one person who owns it. That does not mean one person does everything, but one person holds the plan, makes the decisions and is the single point of contact for the removals company, the landlord and the staff.

Give the move lead the authority to make calls without a committee, and the diary time to do it. On a larger move, pair them with a contact on each floor. Removals Nationwide assigns a move coordinator to every office job, so your move lead has one named counterpart from survey to sign-off.

## 3. Audit and declutter before you pack

Moving is priced by volume. Every cabinet of paper nobody has opened in five years, every broken chair and every redundant printer adds to the crate count, the crew hours and the vehicle size. An audit a few weeks out pays for itself.

Walk every room with a three-way decision for each item: keep, dispose or archive. Shred paper you no longer need. Decommissioned IT should be recycled to WEEE standards, and old furniture can be cleared as part of the same booking.

## 4. Measure the new space and plan the layout

Do not assume the new office will take everything the old one did. Measure the floor, corridors, lift car and door frames against your largest desks, storage units and meeting tables. Finding out on move day that a boardroom table will not fit in the lift is avoidable.

Produce a floor plan of the new office with every desk, pod and storage unit numbered. It tells the crew where everything goes and becomes the basis of your labelling. ## 5. Book building access and lifts at both ends

Managed buildings in London do not simply let a crew turn up. Most require a goods lift booking, a loading bay reservation and often a method statement and risk assessment (RAMS) before access is granted. Some only allow moves out of hours.

Contact building management at both addresses early and find out exactly what they need and when. Confirm lift dimensions and weight limits, whether the lift can be locked off, and the rules on protecting floors and door frames. Pass all of it to your removals company, who will produce the RAMS and liaise with the building team.

## 6. Sort parking and loading in London

A removals vehicle with nowhere legal to stop is a move already behind schedule. In permit zones the council will need to suspend bays outside one or both addresses, and that application typically goes in two to five working days before move day, longer in busy boroughs.

Check whether either address sits inside the Congestion Charge zone or near a School Street with timed restrictions, and plan loading windows accordingly. A good removals company will arrange the suspensions and loading bay bookings, but it needs the addresses and dates in good time.

## 7. Label by zone and desk

A box marked "Office" tells the crew nothing. A crate marked "Floor 2, Zone B, Desk 14" tells them exactly where it goes, and tells the person at Desk 14 which crates are theirs. Use the numbering from your floor plan so labels, plan and new office all match.

Colour-code by department or floor on a large move, and label two sides of every crate so it can be read however it is stacked. Tag each dismantled desk with the workstation it becomes.

## 8. Handle IT separately

IT is the highest-risk part of any office move and decides whether staff can work on day one. Treat it as its own workstream, owned by your IT lead and agreed with the removals company well in advance.

Your IT team should disconnect equipment to their own protocol, label every cable bundle and set the order in which kit is packed and reconnected. Equipment then travels in anti-static IT crates, loaded last and unloaded first, so reconnection can begin before anything else arrives. Back everything up before a single cable is pulled.

## 9. Use crates, not cardboard, for files

Cardboard is fine for a one-way house move, but for an office full of files it is slow to assemble, easy to overload and leaves a mountain of waste. Lidded plastic crates stack securely, keep contents dry, seal with security locks and go back to the supplier when you are done.

A standard LC3 crate holds up to ten lever arch files, so estimating is simple: count the files, divide by ten and add a margin. ## 10. Communicate with staff and clients

People worry about what they do not know. Tell staff early what is happening, when, and what you need from them: which day to clear desks, how to label personal items and where they will sit. A short written briefing a few weeks out, with a reminder the week before, prevents most questions.

Clients and suppliers need to hear from you too. Give them the new address, any dates when phones or email might be interrupted, and a contact for anything urgent.

## 11. Update your address everywhere

Few things undermine a well-run move like a month of post going to the old office. List every place your address appears and work through it: Companies House, HMRC, your bank, insurers, utilities, website and email signatures, Google Business Profile, stationery and contracts.

Set up Royal Mail redirection as a safety net, and update client-facing systems such as invoice templates and online directories on the day the move completes.

## 12. Plan the first day back

The move does not end when the last crate comes off the van. It ends when your team can sit down and work. Plan the first morning as carefully as move day: who arrives early to check IT is live, who directs people to their desks and who deals with the crate on the wrong floor.

Moving over an evening or weekend makes this far easier, because the crew works while the office is empty and staff arrive at a working office on the next business day. Evening and weekend office moves are available 7 days a week for exactly this reason.

## 13. Keep a contingency

Even a well-planned move can be thrown by a lift failure, a late fit-out or a key holder who does not appear. Build a buffer into the timeline, keep a reserve in the budget and know what you will do if the new office is not ready on the agreed date.

Interim [storage](/storage/) is the usual answer. Items not needed in the first phase, or a whole office waiting for its floor to be finished, can be held in a 24/7 CCTV-monitored facility and delivered when the space is ready. Agree that option at the planning stage so it is a phone call rather than a crisis.

## Bringing it together

An office move is a project like any other. Give it a lead, a timeline and a clear plan for access, parking, IT and labelling, and the stress falls away because nothing is left to chance.
